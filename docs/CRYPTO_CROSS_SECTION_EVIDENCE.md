# Crypto cross-section: recent factor evidence and transfer limits

Reviewed 2026-10-19. This note adds recent external evidence that is not covered in detail by the project’s main source catalogue. It is a literature map, not a signal recommendation or a new result for this repository. Most papers study spot assets and ask which assets outperform others; they do not establish a reliable probability that a particular perpetual contract will rise, nor a net executable edge on MEXC.

## Recent evidence

| Study | Design and result reported by the source | What it can support | Boundary for this project |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mercik, Zaremba & Demir, [“Crypto Factor Zoo (.Zip)”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.irfa.2026.105137), *International Review of Financial Analysis* (2026) | Iteratively adds factors to a CAPM using the factor that most reduces the GRS statistic; tests 36 factors across 565 cryptoassets, 2018–2024. The authors report that two or three selected factors remove significant portfolio alphas. | Many published crypto characteristics may compress into a small set of correlated return dimensions; treating every feature as independent confirmation is unsafe. | Full-sample factor-model compression is not a point-in-time forecast validation or a perpetual execution test. Selection can explain variation without producing a stable tradable forecast. |
| Cong, Karolyi, Tang & Zhao, [“Crypto Value, Factor Pricing, and Market Segmentation”](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05875), *Management Science* (online 2026) | Builds an active-addresses/market-cap value factor and a crypto market/size/momentum/value model; reports better cross-sectional fit and segmentation across token functions. Its supplement labels the AA/MC premium in-sample; weekly evidence spans 2017-07-17 to 2024-09-29. | On-chain activity relative to valuation and token category are plausible candidate dimensions. | Active addresses are not unique people, category membership needs point-in-time vintages, and an in-sample cross-sectional premium does not establish an OOS coin-direction forecast. |
| Guidolin & Ionta, [“Predictive sorting of cryptocurrencies based on fundamentals and sentiment”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1042443126000016), *Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions & Money* (2026) | Examines weekly characteristics including active users, hashrate and Google Trends; its initial dataset covers 2015-08-21 to 2025-04-25 (505 weekly observations). | Fundamental and attention variables may add information beyond price-only sorts in a broad crypto cross-section. | Definitions and release timestamps matter; Google Trends and chain metrics are not exchange-native futures features. Its result should not be treated as independent confirmation of price/order-flow signals. |
| Jung & Park, [“Common factors in crypto returns”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.frl.2024.105485), *Finance Research Letters* (2024) | Reports that market, size and momentum factors explain only part of cryptocurrency excess-return variation, with much of the residual variation associated with a common component not fully explained by a broad macro/financial-variable set. | A few familiar factors may not span the main crypto-wide return driver; common shocks can remain. | A latent common component is not itself a usable predictor. Coin rankings can look informative while most assets still move together, so assess both relative ranking and market-direction forecasts. |
| Wang & Pang, [“Predictability on the Move: The Cryptocurrency Cross-Section”](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7556359), SSRN preprint (October 2026) | The abstract reports 1,043 signals and as many as 750,960 signed candidates. StepM selects none over the full sample at 5%; rolling 104-week windows select strategies in 1 of 24 early windows and 24 of 28 later windows, mostly short-horizon liquidity/activity signals. | The search procedure and time period can change the answer; a pooled winner may hide regime-specific behavior. | This is a new preprint, not peer-reviewed evidence. The accessible abstract does not state the full sample dates; the rolling-window result is not proof that the later signals will persist. |

The wider literature suggests useful *feature families*, not a shopping list to add to one model. Sakkas and Urquhart’s [blockchain-factor study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intfin.2024.102012) retains a network-distribution measure based on large-holder address concentration alongside the market factor. Benedetti and Rodríguez-Garnica’s [token-supply-pressure study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jimonfin.2025.103408) measures observed exchange deposits for Ethereum-based tokens; that is distinct from scheduled vesting or future issuance. Yadav’s [liquidity-volatility study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.frl.2026.110859) studies variation in illiquidity, not just its average level. Each is a spot/cross-sectional candidate requiring its own timing, coverage and execution checks.

## How to use this evidence in the repository

The relevant distinction is between (a) explaining cross-sectional return differences, (b) forecasting one coin’s absolute direction, (c) ranking coins relative to the universe, and (d) earning net returns through an executable venue. A paper can support (a) without supporting (b)–(d). The repo’s current discovery and holdout results are chiefly portfolio/ranking evidence; do not convert a long–short spread into a calibrated “probability this coin goes up.”

Before exploring these features in a new lane:

1. Define the mechanism and timestamp separately: future issuance, already-minted token unlock, current circulating supply, observed exchange deposit pressure, active-address intensity, holder concentration, or liquidity volatility are not interchangeable. Store the observation time and the first time it could have been known; freeze revised historical data by vintage where possible.
2. Declare whether the target is an absolute return, excess return versus a fixed benchmark, or a cross-sectional rank. Set one horizon and one primary score. Report universe coverage, including missing, newly listed, delisted and venue-ineligible assets.
3. Test one primary feature family against a frozen baseline. Keep exploratory variants in the recorded trial family; do not count correlated features as separate confirmations. Evaluate a fresh forward period after the specification is frozen.
4. Attribute any result across market, size, momentum, liquidity, volatility and funding exposures using definitions fixed before seeing the outcome. Measure tradability on the actual contract venue at planned notional; spot-factor papers do not supply perpetual fills, fees or margin behavior.

The repository already has an in-sample token-unlock finding and an audit warning that its flat cost assumption is liquidity-blind ([AUDIT.md](AUDIT.md)). New supply or liquidity measures are therefore hypotheses to distinguish and stress, not independent replications of that result. Leave existing frozen lanes and live forward specifications untouched; register any future measurement under a new lane before running it.

## Synthetic check

A separate synthetic fixture verified the accounting distinction between supply growth and unlocks. With price $2, maximum supply 200 and circulating supply 100, market cap is $200, fully diluted value is $400 and FDV/market cap is 2. Issuing 10 new tokens implies 10% supply growth. Unlocking 20 already-minted tokens is 20% of current circulation but leaves current circulating and total supply unchanged until the eligibility date; afterward circulation is 120 and total supply remains 200. Moving the disclosure timestamp after the signal cutoff removes the feature at that cutoff. Seven arithmetic and point-in-time assertions passed. This uses invented inputs and says nothing about returns or a market edge.
